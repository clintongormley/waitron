B3 — THE BOOTABLE USB INSTALLER: FIVE QUESTIONS, ONE AT A TIME
Design: docs/superpowers/specs/2026-10-21-b3-usb-installer-design.md (§13). Pictures in this folder.
Ask 1, then 2, 3, 4, 5. Each has a recommendation; the last option is always "Stop here — I'll write".

In short: the stick boots a small installer that copies a ready-made Waitron box (Debian 13, Docker,
the release's Waitron images) onto the mini PC's disk, then restarts into the setup wizard. CI builds
the stick for every release and installs it in a virtual machine before publishing it. Both of a
venue's boxes (A947) come from the same stick; the wizard asks which job each does.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
QUESTION 1 of 5 — what the stick does
Pictures: 7-q1-what-the-stick-does.png (also 1-this-box.png and 2-venue-found.png)

Example: Monday 10:00, the deli's new box arrives; the shop's internet line is not connected until
Wednesday.

A (recommended) — the stick carries everything and asks once. About 15 minutes (estimate; measured
  in the first build), no internet needed. The box's screen shows the disk and what it holds
  ("Windows"), and one press on «Borrar e instalar» starts it. If the box already holds a Waitron
  restaurant, the screen names it («Bar Santet», Live, last sale 20/10/2026 23:41, 3 records not yet
  sent to AEAT), says its own backups go with the disk, and the button works only after the
  restaurant's name is typed. This is weaker than `waitron.sh`, which refuses to wipe a Live box; it
  follows your A732 answer that a broken box is reinstalled. The deli reaches the setup wizard by about 10:15 and can
  prepare its menu before the line arrives. Cost: a large download per release for whoever writes
  the stick; a box with no screen needs one plugged in for the install.
B — a small stick that downloads the release during the install. Always the newest release, but the
  deli waits until Wednesday or borrows a phone's connection for a download of several gigabytes.
C — fully unattended: like A, but it erases and installs with no question, so a box with no screen
  can be installed. A stick left in a box that restarts after a power cut can then erase a working
  restaurant.

QUESTION 2 of 5 — a full-screen browser on every box (your lean of 2026-09-29)
Pictures: 5-box-screen.png

Example: the deli's box sits in the cupboard under the counter, with the counter touchscreen on top.

A (recommended) — every box carries the full-screen browser (Chromium under `cage`, no desktop) and
  shows it whenever a screen is plugged in: the setup wizard first, then question 3's screen. Cost:
  about a gigabyte of the box's memory while it runs (reasoning, not measured), and Chromium updates
  from Debian every few weeks.
B — a server-only box. The box's screen shows a text line with its address; tills are always
  separate machines or tablets. Smaller and simpler; the deli needs a second machine for its
  counter (the hardware notes price one at €300–450).

QUESTION 3 of 5 — is the box's own screen a till? (your lean of 2026-09-29)
Pictures: 5-box-screen.png

Example: the deli has one counter. Its box drives the counter screen. Later it adds a spare box (A947)
in the back room, with an old monitor on it.

A (recommended) — the box's screen joins like any other till: a manager accepts it once, and staff
  sign in with their PIN (no Linux login; the PIN is the lock). On the spare box, the same screen is
  a till of the main box, so if the main box dies, the back-room screen keeps working once the spare
  takes over. If the main box dies, the counter screen it drives dies with it (A947 §9 says so).
B — the box's screen never sells. It shows the box's state: restaurant, selling or waiting, address,
  spare up to date, last copy. The counter needs its own till machine.

QUESTION 4 of 5 — a maintenance login on the box (the 2026-09-11 discussion record)
Pictures: 8-q4-maintenance.png

Example: a Friday night, the box will not start Waitron; Waitron's support person is at the venue
with a keyboard and wants to look at the box before wiping it.

A (recommended) — a `maintenance` account with no standing password. Booting the stick and choosing
  «Abrir acceso de mantenimiento» shows a fresh six-word password valid until the box next restarts;
  the person removes the stick, restarts, and signs in at the box's screen. No SSH. Anyone who has
  the box and a stick can do this — but anyone with the box can already read its disk today.
B — a password per box, set when it is installed and printed on a sealed card kept with the box.
  Works without the stick; a lost card means a reinstall, or a procedure not yet designed.
C — no login at all: every repair is a reinstall from the stick, then a restore from backup.

QUESTION 5 of 5 — Wi-Fi for the box ("AP-mode WiFi onboarding" in the backlog)
Pictures: 9-q5-wifi.png (also 3-network-clock.png)

Example: a market stall with no cable to its router.

A (recommended) — the box is wired whenever possible (A947 puts both boxes on one switch). With no
  cable, the installer's own screen lists the Wi-Fi networks and asks for the password. Changing the
  Wi-Fi later (a new router) needs a reinstall until a later item adds a way.
B — the box also opens its own Wi-Fi hotspot «Waitron-4F2A», with a password unique to that box,
  whenever it has no cable and no known network; a phone joins it, a page asks for the venue's
  Wi-Fi, and the hotspot closes. Useful only for a box with neither screen nor cable. Intel Wi-Fi
  cards, common in mini PCs, run a hotspot on 2.4 GHz only.
